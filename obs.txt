This script is running an **Iterative Learning Control (ILC)** experiment on a simulated 2-joint robotic arm (a "2R" arm). Iterative Learning Control is a method where a robot repeats the same task over and over (the trials), learning from its mistakes in previous trials to improve its performance in the next one.

Here is a breakdown of exactly what is happening in the MuJoCo viewer and what the charts mean.

---

### 1. What's Happening in the MuJoCo Viewer
In the viewer, you are watching the 2R arm attempt to reach a specific target angle: **45 degrees ($\frac{\pi}{4}$ radians)** for both joints (`Theta1` and `Theta2`). 

The simulation runs for **1000 consecutive trials**, and the script splits these trials into 4 distinct "Phases" to test how well the learning algorithm adapts to unexpected physical changes:

1. **BaseLine Phase (Trials 1-400):** The robot learns how to move to the 45-degree angle in a normal environment. Over these trials, you will see its movement get smoother and more accurate as the ILC algorithm fine-tunes the motor torques.
2. **Adaptation Phase (Trials 401-600):** A sudden physical disturbance is introduced. Specifically, the script adds a constant negative force (`perturbation = -1`) to the first motor. It's like a sudden strong wind blowing against the arm. The robot will initially fail to reach the target, but over these 200 trials, the algorithm will adapt and learn to push harder against the "wind".
3. **Washout Phase (Trials 601-800):** The "wind" is suddenly turned off. Because the robot was pushing hard to counteract the wind, it will initially overshoot the target. It must now "unlearn" the adaptation and go back to its baseline torques.
4. **Readaptation Phase (Trials 801-1000):** The "wind" is turned back on. This tests a phenomenon in motor learning called "savings"—the robot should theoretically adapt to the wind much faster this time because it has seen it before.

---

### 2. The Charts Explained

Once the simulation finishes, the script generates three charts using `matplotlib`.

#### Chart 1: Trial Number vs. Norm of Error (Stem Plot)
- **X-axis:** The Trial Number (1 to 1000).
- **Y-axis:** The physical error (the distance between where the arm actually ended up vs. the 45-degree target).
- **What it shows:** This is the overall "Learning Curve". 
  - You will see the error start high at Trial 1 and quickly drop toward zero as it learns during the **Baseline**.
  - At Trial 401, you will see a massive spike in error because the perturbation was turned on. The error will then slowly drop back down as it adapts.
  - You will see another spike at Trial 601 (when the perturbation is removed) and a final spike at Trial 801 (when it is added back). 

#### Chart 2 & 3: Theta1 & Theta2 Across Time Steps (Line Plots)
- **X-axis:** The Time Step within a *single* trial (0 to 1000).
- **Y-axis:** The angle of the joint in radians.
- **Red Line:** The target angle ($\frac{\pi}{4}$ or roughly 0.785 radians).
- **What it shows:** Your script is specifically capturing the moment-to-moment movement of the arm during **Trials 150 through 160**. 
  - Each colored line represents the trajectory of the arm for one of those specific trials.
  - Because trials 150-160 happen well into the Baseline phase, the algorithm has already learned quite a bit. You will see these lines tracking very closely to the red target line, visually proving that the arm is successfully stabilizing at 45 degrees.

### 2. Conceptual Role: The "Fast" Brain
In biology and robotics, this is often called the **Explicit Strategy**. 
*   **It is reactive:** It looks at the error and immediately tries to come up with a torque to fix it.

This is exactly how humans react to a sudden change—we adapt fast, but we also "unlearn" fast when the change goes away.

These parameters are the "dials" that control the behavior of the **Dual-Rate Learning** framework. They determine how the robot balances reacting quickly to changes versus remembering things long-term.

Here is how each one affects the simulation:

### 1. `lr=0.05` (Learning Rate)
*   **The Effect:** This controls the "speed" of the **Fast Process**. 
*   **In the Simulation:** When the "wind" (perturbation) starts at Trial 401, a high `lr` allows the neural network to change its output drastically in just 1 or 2 trials.
*   **If you increase it:** The robot will adapt to the wind almost instantly, but it might become "jittery" or unstable.
*   **If you decrease it:** The robot will take many more trials to correct its error during the Adaptation phase.

### 2. `weight_decay=0.1` (Forgetting Factor)
*   **The Effect:** This forces the neural network's weights to constantly shrink toward zero if they aren't being actively "pushed" by an error.
*   **In the Simulation:** This is what makes the process **"Fast but fragile."** During the **Washout** phase (when the wind stops), this high weight decay causes the neural network to "forget" its counter-force strategy very rapidly.
*   **Why it's important:** Without this, the neural network would hold onto the old strategy for too long, causing the robot to keep pushing against a wind that is no longer there.

### 3. `alpha = 0.5` (Blending Factor)
*   **The Effect:** This is the "Attention" or "Trust" parameter. It decides how much of the final motor torque comes from the **Fast Neural Network** vs. the **Slow ILC**.
*   **The Math:** $Torque = (\alpha \times Fast) + ((1 - \alpha) \times Slow)$
*   **If `alpha = 0.9`:** The robot relies almost entirely on its reactive neural network. It will be very fast to adapt but might look "nervous" or less smooth.
*   **If `alpha = 0.1`:** The robot relies mostly on its slow, steady motor memory (ILC). It will be very smooth and stable, but it will be very slow to react when the perturbation starts.

### How they work together:
In the **Dual-Rate** theory of the brain:
1.  **Fast Process (`alpha` high, `lr` high, `weight_decay` high):** Handles the "Quick Fix" when something changes suddenly.
2.  **Slow Process (`alpha` low, `lr` low, `weight_decay` low):** Slowly "takes over" the learning from the fast process to store it as permanent motor memory. 

By setting `alpha = 0.5`, you are allowing both systems to work together: the Neural Network fixes the big errors quickly, while the ILC slowly builds up a stable, permanent memory of the correct movement.
